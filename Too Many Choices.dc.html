One try per grid size. Attention, luck, your screen and where the target landed all play a part.

More isn’t always better. But less isn’t always easier.

Why thirty felt harder than four.

Visual search. Four symbols you take in at a glance. With thirty, your eyes move, settle, compare and move again.

Lookalikes slow you down. A target that differs in one simple way pops out of any crowd. One that shares features with its neighbors takes longer as the grid grows (Treisman & Gelade, 1980).

The Hick–Hyman law. Choice time grows with the logarithm of the number of options (Hick, 1952; Hyman, 1953). This task adds a search step, so it speaks to clutter more than to the law.

How this was built
What it measures
Time from the grid appearing to a correct tap, incorrect taps, and how difficult each round felt. Grid size (4, 12 or 30 symbols) is the only thing that changes, and the round order is random.
What it isn’t
A test of the Hick–Hyman law, which describes choosing a response from a known set. Most of your time here went to finding the target.
Limitations
An informal demonstration, not a validated study. Each grid size runs once, so a lucky glance can change a result. Screen size, input device, target position, shape familiarity and round order all play a part. Randomization reduces these effects without removing them.
