Overview

I designed an AAC platform that gives children with cerebral palsy a voice through play

Over the summer of 2025 I led SayPlay in partnership with the United Cerebral Palsy Center of Greater Cleveland. Existing AAC tools ask a child to hunt through a dense grid of symbols with an adult beside them; almost none of them carry the vocabulary for how that child feels. I spent eight weeks on site to understand why, then designed a platform around play, emotion and connection.

Problem

Existing AAC tools weren't built for how young children with cerebral palsy actually think and move

Children with cerebral palsy often experience significant speech and motor impairments that make verbal communication difficult or impossible. While AAC tools exist to support expressive language, many are designed without considering the unique cognitive, physical, and emotional needs of young children with motor impairments.

This results in tools that are unintuitive, disengaging, and difficult for children and caregivers to use consistently. There is a pressing need for an accessible, adaptable, and engaging AAC platform that empowers children with cerebral palsy to express themselves more independently and meaningfully — both at home and in therapy settings.

Design system

A library built for large targets and high contrast

Every component was sized for a child with limited fine motor control first: oversized targets, generous spacing, and a palette that stays legible at low contrast sensitivity. The four avatars carry the personality so the interface itself can stay quiet.

Final design

The main board, as a child and a caregiver both see it

One screen carries the whole interaction. Boards stay fixed down the left so a child learns their position by muscle memory rather than by reading. The sentence builds left to right along the top, where a caregiver leaning over the tablet can read it without blocking the grid. Everything else recedes.

Sentence strip

Each word stays a tappable chip after it lands, so a wrong pick is corrected by touching it rather than clearing the line and starting over.

Board rail

Five boards, always in the same order, each with a fixed colour. Position and colour do the identifying work so the labels are a convenience rather than a requirement.

Grid

Fifteen tiles per page rather than a dense board. Targets clear the motor-control threshold therapists set, and the selected tile holds a visible ring so a child can see what they chose.

Progress row

Kept factual and placed at the bottom edge. It is there for the caregiver reviewing a session, not as a reward loop competing with the child's attention.
