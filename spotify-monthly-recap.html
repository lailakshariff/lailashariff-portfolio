Spotify Chapters

A year of listening, kept as twelve smaller stories

{{ m.k }}
{{ m.v }}
Spotify Wrapped has made reflecting on a year of listening feel like an event. But our relationship with music doesn't happen once a year. I designed Chapters to explore what music reflection could look like at a more personal, monthly scale, pairing listening data with the photographs from that same period.
January

We remember the year. What about everything in between?

Spotify Wrapped arrives once a year, but songs become attached to commutes, trips, friendships, late nights, new cities, ordinary Tuesdays, and entire phases of our lives. Most of those smaller listening eras disappear into our history.

From data to memory
Designing beyond the numbers
Listening data is useful, but statistics alone don't always explain why a song mattered. I wanted the experience to sit somewhere between data visualisation and memory keeping. Personal imagery provides the context; together they turn passive listening history into something emotionally meaningful and worth returning to.
Each chapter is built from six ingredients, three from the platform and three from the person.
{{ g }}

Every month becomes a tiny time capsule. Part recap, part scrapbook.

Chapters transforms each month of listening into a visual memory archive. Instead of focusing only on statistics, the experience brings together what you listened to, what defined the month, and what you want to remember. Users can revisit favourite artists, albums, tracks and listening patterns alongside their own photographs from the same weeks.

A story, not a dashboard.

Rather than presenting the month as one dense analytics screen, I structured the recap as a sequence of smaller moments. Each section reveals another part of the listener's month, so the experience unfolds like a story rather than a report.

Consistent structure. Different story.

The hierarchy and components stay fixed. Artwork, photography and listening data change the character of every recap, so the system feels familiar without making each month feel identical.
The same structure, twelve times over. Only the material changes.
Designed across devices
One experience, two rhythms
I designed the concept across mobile and desktop rather than treating desktop as an enlarged phone. On mobile the experience is intimate and sequential, designed for tapping, scrolling and moving quickly through memories. On desktop the additional space lets content, imagery and listening data become more editorial and immersive. The underlying story stays consistent while the composition adapts to how each device is used.
Mobile, same chapter.

January sounded different from June. June felt different from October.

The larger idea extends beyond any individual month. Over time, Chapters becomes a growing archive of listening eras: a way to look back and remember not only which songs were on repeat, but the life happening around them. Together, those months tell a story that an annual summary alone can't fully capture.
Live prototype

Designed to unfold. Click anywhere inside it.

Motion helps turn the recap from a collection of statistics into an experience. I prototyped interactions across mobile and desktop to explore how transitions, scrolling, layering and progressive reveals could guide someone through the month without overwhelming them. The goal was not motion for decoration, but motion that creates pacing and makes each part of the recap feel intentionally revealed.

December

Behavioural data as an emotional material.

This project gave me an opportunity to explore how behavioural data can become an emotional design material. The most interesting challenge wasn't deciding which statistics to show. It was thinking about what could make those statistics meaningful enough for someone to want to return to them. Pairing listening behaviour with personal imagery shifted the experience from “What did I listen to?” toward “What did this part of my life feel like?” That distinction became the foundation of the concept.
Working within a known system
Designing inside a product people already know meant the interface had to feel familiar before it felt new. The structure carries the recognition; the content carries the difference.
If I took it further…
I'd test whether people actually revisit a past month, which is the claim the whole concept rests on, and design what a chapter looks like when someone adds no photographs at all.
Your year isn't one story. It's twelve of them.
